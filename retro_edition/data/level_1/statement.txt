[Time] 5th October, 1988 | 2:36 AM
[Location] House 17, Block B, Green Road, Gulshan, Dhaka
[Statement]
For six months, journalist Arif Rahman had been following a story that his editor repeatedly told him to abandon.
It began with dead cats.
They appeared first around Palashi. Then Azimpur. Then the narrow alleys behind old warehouses near the Buriganga. Their bodies were never simply mutilated. Their eyes were crushed, sometimes removed entirely, and strange cuts were found across their bodies. There was no obvious pattern to the killings except their cruelty.
Arif believed there was one.
He began collecting reports that had never reached the newspapers. Missing animals. Missing people. Witnesses who suddenly refused to speak. Names that appeared repeatedly in unrelated accounts. Eventually, he gave the investigation a name of his own.
EVIL EYE'S WRATH.
Nobody knew whether that was the name of an organization, a ritual, or something Arif had invented to make sense of the evidence.
His wife, Sultana, knew only that the investigation was consuming him. For weeks, Arif had barely slept. He kept a notebook containing everything he had discovered and refused to let anyone see it. The notebook was kept inside a steel vault in his study.
On the night of 5th October, Arif went to bed at approximately midnight. At 2:36 AM, the electricity to the house was cut. The house went completely dark.
A few minutes later, Sultana woke to the sound of something heavy striking the floor. Then she heard her husband's voice.
Not shouting. Begging.
She ran toward the study. What she saw there would destroy her ability to speak for months.
Three men were inside the room. Arif was fighting one of them. Another was standing beside the steel vault. The third was holding a long knife.
And Sultana knew all three of them. They were her husband's colleagues. Arif saw her. For one brief moment, their eyes met.
Then the knife went into him.
Sultana screamed.
Someone struck her across the head.
The room disappeared.
--- 10:57 AM ---
When she opened her eyes again, she was lying beside her husband's body.
The men were gone. So was the notebook. The scene they left behind told a different story.
The study had been rearranged. The knife had been placed near Sultana's hand.
Arif's desk had been overturned. Several pages had been torn from his investigation. There were signs of a violent struggle between husband and wife. It looked as though Arif had attacked Sultana during an argument, and she had killed him while defending herself. It was almost convincing.
Almost. Because whoever staged the room made one mistake.
They forgot that Sultana was still alive.
She was taken to hospital with severe head trauma. She survived. But when the doctors asked her what happened, she could not speak.
Days passed.
Then weeks.
The investigation began collapsing under its own contradictions. Seven people emerged as possible suspects.
Three had been seen near the house. Two had lied about where they were. One had access to Arif's investigation. And one insisted he had never even known the notebook existed.
Then, on the seventh day after the murder, Sultana asked for a piece of paper. Her hands were trembling.
She wrote:
"His colleagues killed him."
Underneath it, after several minutes of hesitation, she wrote:
"They wanted the notebook."
The police asked her what was inside it. She stared at the question for a long time. Then she wrote:
"EVIL EYE'S WRATH."
A second line followed.
"They tried to erase the investigation."
She suddenly became agitated. The nurse tried to take the paper away. Sultana refused to let go. She wrote one final sentence before collapsing:
"One of them was lying about what happened to Arif."
She never explained which one. Her condition deteriorated that night. Three days later, she was shown photographs of the seven suspects.
She stopped at the photograph of Durjo Das.
She stared at it for nearly a minute. Then she looked away.
Later, when shown Jawer Chakrovarty, her reaction was completely different.
She began shaking. She pointed at his photograph. And tried to point her finger at him, mumbling:
"HIM."
The case changed direction.
Jawer had already admitted that he was near the house that night. He claimed he had arrived after hearing Arif was in danger. He claimed he never touched him. He claimed the knife was already on the floor. His story was believable. Until the police found out that Jawer owned a knife almost identical to the murder weapon.
Then another problem appeared.
The wound on Arif's body did not quite fit the story. The knife had injured him badly. But it had not killed him.
Something else had.
A substance had entered Arif's bloodstream shortly before his death. A poison.
There was no bottle. No vial. No obvious source. Only a tiny puncture mark on Arif's upper arm that investigators had initially dismissed as insignificant.
Suddenly, the question was no longer simply:
Who stabbed Arif Rahman?
It became:
Who needed everyone to believe that he had been stabbed to death?
[Question]
Who actually killed Mr. Arif?
