[Time] 16th November, 1887 | 8:47 AM
[Location] Dhaka Bureaucrat, Dhaka
[Statement]
The notebook was recovered from Jawer Chakrovarty on the tenth of November, 1887. It was found alongside a rhombus-shaped pendant bearing the carving of an eye, crossed out by a deep, deliberate stroke.
Jawer had finally stopped denying his involvement in the theft. He still refused to explain the pendant. When questioned about the Evil Eye's Wrath, he offered only one sentence:
"You think we worshipped it. You have mistaken the direction of the bargain."
Before the investigator could press him further, Jawer began to laugh. Not loudly. Not wildly. It was the quiet, exhausted laughter of a man who had just watched someone walk willingly into a trap.
That evening, the investigator carried the notebook and pendant to his private chamber. He placed the pendant on his desk and locked the door from within. He checked the windows, inspected the fireplace, and pushed a heavy cabinet against the only other entrance. He kept the key beneath his pillow.
He remembers doing all of this.
He remembers lying awake.
He remembers hearing someone turn the pages of a book.
But he had placed the notebook inside a locked drawer.
At precisely three in the morning, a voice whispered from the other side of the wall.
"Have you found the right place?"
The investigator reached for his pistol. The voice stopped.
He waited until dawn before unlocking the chamber.
The notebook remained inside the drawer. The pendant remained on the desk.
Beside it lay a folded slip of paper.
There was no sign of forced entry. The dust on the windowsill was undisturbed. The key was still beneath his pillow. The cabinet had not moved.
The message contained seven words:
THE FIRST WITNESS WAS NEVER ARIF.
The investigator assumed the message referred to an unknown informant. Someone who had witnessed the events before Arif began his investigation. Someone whose testimony had been concealed—or whose very existence had been erased.
Then he remembered the anonymous letter.
It had been the letter that first directed him toward Durjo Das. Yet its handwriting was his own.
He rushed to his casebook.
The first entry concerning Durjo had been written before the letter had supposedly arrived. He checked the ink, the paper, the binding, and the dates in his own handwriting. The ink appeared older than the paper on which the entry had been made.
He checked the archive's register. The original letter was missing.
He searched his desk, his coat, his locked drawers, and the pockets of every garment he owned.
Nothing.
When he returned to his casebook, he noticed that the binding was thicker than he remembered. A thin page had been concealed inside its spine.
The handwriting was Arif Rahman's.
"If you are reading this, Jawer has already begun.
Do not trust the order in which you remember these events.
The first witness was never Arif.
It was you."
Below the passage, someone had written a final line in fresh black ink:
YOU HAVE ALREADY FOUND WHAT YOU SEEK.
The investigator had seen those words before. They had appeared in his casebook, and they were associated with the unnatural circumstances surrounding Arindom Kumar Biswas's death. Yet no witness could identify who had written them.
He searched the casebook for the phrase.
It appeared three times.
The first occurrence was dated before Arif's murder.
The second was dated the day Arindom died.
The third bore tomorrow's date.
He tore the page out.
When he looked again, it was still inside the book.
He burned it in the fireplace.
The paper turned black, curled inward, and became ash. But on the following morning, the same page was back inside the binding, pristine and untouched.
The investigator did not tell anyone.
Instead, he began examining the notebook. Under ordinary light, its pages were blank. Under the oblique glow of a candle, however, faint impressions emerged—lines, angles, and shapes pressed into the paper from some invisible source. They did not resemble ordinary writing. They looked more like fragments of a plan.
He could not decipher them.
At the bottom of the final page, a sentence had appeared in his own handwriting:
"I killed Jawer Chakrovarty on the fourteenth of October, 1887."
The investigator stared at the sentence until the letters blurred.
Jawer was alive when he had last seen him.
Or so the official record claimed.
A knock sounded at the chamber door.
Three times.
"Who is there?" the investigator asked.
A familiar voice answered.
"Jawer Chakrovarty."
The investigator moved toward the door.
"I believe you have something of mine," the voice continued.
He unlocked the door.
Nobody stood outside.
On the floor lay a second notebook. Its cover was stained with old blood, and its title was written in Arif's handwriting:
THE HOLLOW CIPHER.
The investigator opened it.
Every page was blank.
Except the final one.
On it, in the same handwriting as the sentence in his own casebook, were the words:
YOU ARE NOT THE FIRST TO READ THIS.
The clock struck three.
Somewhere inside the walls, a man began to pray.
The investigator fled the chamber before sunrise.
He returned with two witnesses. The room was empty. The notebook had vanished. The pendant was back inside the locked drawer.
One witness swore that the investigator had never left the chamber that night.
The other insisted that he had seen him walking through the archive's eastern corridor at three in the morning.
Both men were sober. Both men were certain.
Neither could explain why the corridor's dust contained only one set of footprints.
The investigator's.
They led away from his chamber.
They ended at a wall.
Behind that wall, according to the building's current plans, there was nothing.
But according to an older plan, there had once been a room.
And in the margin, beside that room, someone had written a name that did not appear in any personnel register:
THE CARTOGRAPHER.
No one could establish who had written it.
The handwriting matched the investigator's.
It also matched the anonymous letter.
It also matched neither.
The next morning, a new entry appeared in the casebook:
"The Cartographer has been informed that the search has begun."
The investigator did not remember writing it.
He did not remember hearing the name before the previous night.
Yet when he searched the archive, he found a record of a man by that name who had surveyed the building decades earlier. The record stated that the surveyor had died before the archive was constructed.
A second record, in the same ledger, stated that he had been employed there the previous week.
The signatures were identical. The dates were authentic. The man could not have been alive for both.
And yet, the archive's accounts showed that he had been paid twice.
The investigator began to understand the shape of the trap. Someone—or something—was arranging events so that every explanation remained possible, and every conclusion remained incomplete.
The Cartographer might be a man. He might be a name used by several people. He might be a fabrication that had acquired a paper trail.
Or he might have been the one arranging the paper trail all along.
The investigator could not prove which possibility was true. What he could prove was that the old plan contained a room that no longer existed. And the notebook's faint impressions seemed to correspond to its dimensions.
For the first time since Arif's death, the investigator had something that could be searched for.
Not a murderer. Not a confession. A place.
The recovered notebook contains no readable words, yet its pages bear impressions of writing that seems to have been pressed into them from a hand that never touched the paper. When aligned with the strange geometry etched into the pendant, the marks resemble a blueprint—one that appears in an architectural plan older than the archive itself.
The plan reveals a chamber beneath the building’s eastern wing. Its entrance was erased from every later drawing, replaced by a wall for which no construction record exists. The building has never changed its dimensions, yet the chamber seems to have vanished from its own history. Three witnesses recall a door there; two insist the wall has always been solid. The oldest record lists the chamber’s architect as a man who died before the archive was built.
Last night, a photograph of the eastern wall appeared inside the locked casebook. There is a door in the image. Standing before it is a figure whose face resembles the investigator’s—but whose shadow falls toward a light that does not exist. Beneath the photograph, in fresh ink, are six words:
You have been here before.

[Question]
Where does the hidden chamber lie?
