[Evidences]
[ The Notebook ]
The notebook recovered from Jawer appears blank under ordinary light. Under angled candlelight, shallow impressions become visible across several pages. They contain repeated lines, intersecting angles, and shapes resembling measurements.
The final page contains the investigator's handwriting, including a statement claiming that he killed Jawer on the same date the notebook was recovered.
No ink or pigment has been identified in the faint impressions. The marks are consistent with pressure applied to the paper, but the source of that pressure remains unknown.
When the notebook is placed over the oldest surviving architectural plan of the newspaper archive, several of its geometric impressions align with the dimensions of a room that is absent from every later plan.
The notebook does not reveal what the room contains.
It appears to indicate where the room once stood.
[ The Pendant ]
The pendant is a dark metal rhombus bearing an engraved eye crossed by a single deep line. Its design is unlike the ordinary religious jewellery catalogued in the archive.
A narrow groove runs along one edge. Under magnification, the groove contains particles of the same pale mineral found in the mortar of the archive's oldest surviving foundation.
The pendant was recovered from Jawer, who admitted that it belonged to him but refused to explain its significance.
The investigator initially considered it a token of the Evil Eye's Wrath. However, the carving is not simply an eye that has been crossed out. The incision begins outside the engraved boundary and ends beyond it, as though the mark was intended to indicate a direction rather than erase a symbol.
The direction is difficult to determine without a reference point.
[ The Anonymous Letter ]
The letter that initiated the investigator's inquiry into Durjo Das cannot be found in the archive. Its arrival is documented in the investigator's own notes, but no independent record confirms that it was ever delivered.
A copy of its text survives in the casebook.
The handwriting matches several entries made by the investigator, including an entry dated before the letter's supposed arrival. Ink analysis suggests that the writing predates the paper, although no reliable method has established the exact sequence.
The investigator insists that he did not write the letter.
No other person has been identified as its author.
[ The Impossible Page ]
A page concealed inside the binding of the investigator's casebook contains a message in Arif's handwriting. The page refers to Jawer and warns against trusting the order of remembered events.
The paper is of a type used by the newspaper office during Arif's employment. The ink resembles that used in his surviving notes.
The page has no date, signature, or indication of how it entered the binding.
The investigator claims that he removed the page and burned it. Two witnesses later examined the page in the casebook and found no evidence of fire damage.
Neither witness saw the investigator burn it.
Both insist he told them he had.
[ The Cartographer's Record ]
An old ledger contains an entry for a surveyor identified as the Cartographer. The record attributes to him a survey of the newspaper archive's grounds, including an enclosed chamber beneath its eastern wing.
The ledger places his death before the archive's construction. A separate entry records a payment to the same name in the current year.
The two signatures are indistinguishable.
The payment is confirmed by the archive's accounts, but the identity of the recipient cannot be established. No employee remembers a surveyor visiting the premises.
The name does not appear in the official staff register.
[ The Altered Plans ]
The earliest architectural plan of the newspaper archive shows an enclosed chamber beneath the eastern wing. Its walls do not correspond to any currently accessible room.
A later plan omits the chamber and redistributes the surrounding measurements. The alteration is not signed, and the archive contains no construction order authorizing the change.
The current building's exterior dimensions remain consistent with the oldest plan. The missing chamber's area appears to have been incorporated into the structure rather than removed from it.
The notebook's impressions align with the chamber's dimensions. The pendant's mineral residue is consistent with the old foundation's mortar.
These findings establish a physical connection between the objects and the altered section of the building. They do not establish what the chamber was used for, who sealed it, or whether it remains accessible.
[ The Three Witnesses ]
Three people were questioned about the investigator's movements on the night the second notebook appeared.
The first stated that the investigator remained inside his locked chamber until morning.
The second claimed to have seen him in the eastern corridor at approximately three o'clock.
The third remembered hearing a voice speaking from inside the wall, followed by the sound of someone knocking three times.
Their statements agree on the time but conflict on the investigator's location.
The corridor contained one set of footprints, belonging to the investigator. They led toward the wall behind which the missing chamber should have been located.
No footprints led away.
The witnesses were interviewed separately and did not communicate with one another before giving their statements.
[ The Photographer's Plate ]
Among the surviving photographs of the newspaper archive is a plate showing the eastern wing before its records were altered. The image contains a narrow structural irregularity near the foundation, almost hidden by shadow.
The plate bears no reliable date. Its catalogue entry identifies the photographer, but the name is partly obscured by damage.
The image has not been conclusively matched to any known stage of construction.
When compared with the oldest architectural plan, the irregularity appears to correspond to the sealed chamber's approximate position.
No one has established whether the photograph records a genuine entrance, an architectural defect, or a distortion caused by the exposure process.
