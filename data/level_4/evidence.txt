[Evidences]
[ The National Ledger ]
The ledger is bound in black leather and contains hundreds of entries arranged by district rather than by date. Each entry identifies a local intermediary, a planned incident, the rumour to be spread afterward, and the person responsible for ensuring that the rumour reached the public.
The incidents were designed to appear unrelated. In one town, a series of deaths would be blamed on a cursed well. In another, a child would survive an illness after a stranger placed a black stone beneath the bed. Elsewhere, a judge would receive letters describing private acts he had never confessed. The details differed, but the method was identical. First came an inexplicable event. Then came a witness. Then came a story. Finally, someone appeared who claimed to understand the meaning of the event and offered protection from its repetition.
The ledger records payments to printers, travelling photographers, apothecaries, private guards, and men who delivered sealed envelopes without asking what they contained. Several payments were disguised as fees for portraits and documentary work. Others were recorded as expenses for the newspaper archive.
The most disturbing entries are not those that describe successful operations. They are the entries marked with a small, empty circle. Those locations were never crossed out, abandoned, or declared unsuccessful. They were marked as already begun.
The same mark appears beside the archive itself.
At the end of the ledger is a list of names. Some belong to known members of the Evil Eye's Wrath. Others belong to people who publicly denounced the cult. A few belong to people who died before the ledger was supposedly created. The final name is the investigator's.
Beside it is a single instruction.
DO NOT RECRUIT. LET THE INVESTIGATION DO IT.
[ The Three Curses ]
A separate document describes the fates of Arif, Arindom, and Jawer. It does not call them murders, punishments, or accidents. It calls them corrections.
Arif Rahman discovered that the Evil Eye's Wrath was manufacturing the evidence that sustained its influence. He believed the notebook would expose the network. The document records that he was to be silenced before he could publish his findings. His death on 5 October 1887 was made to resemble an ordinary violent crime, concealing the poison that actually killed him. The knife wound was a message for the living, not the cause of death.
Arindom Kumar Biswas helped suppress the investigation and believed that his cooperation would spare him from the consequences. After his arrest, he died in custody with his eyes torn out and his body arranged in a posture resembling prayer. The document states that he had asked the Eye to hide him from what he had done. His eyes were taken not simply as mutilation, but as a final answer to that request.
Jawer Chakrovarty stole the notebook because he believed its contents could make him indispensable to the cult. His name appears beside a phrase written in a different hand: He who steals the key shall become its lock. Jawer had not merely taken evidence. He had accepted a ritual obligation that made the stolen notebook part of his own punishment.
The document contains no explanation of how the curses were carried out. Some instructions are practical. Others describe events that should be impossible. One passage orders a witness to be present in two places on the same night. Another records that a man will remember a death before the victim has been chosen.
At the bottom of the page, beneath the three names, is a fourth space.
The investigator's name has been written there, then carefully scratched away.
The pressure of the writing remains visible.
[ The Photographer's Archive ]
The photographic plates were catalogued by date, location, subject, and exposure. Many show ordinary scenes: newspaper offices, street vendors, courtrooms, private gatherings, and portraits of people who had no apparent connection to the cult.
When arranged by the time of exposure, however, the photographs reveal a pattern. The same man appears at the edge of events across several years. Sometimes he is standing beside a window. Sometimes he is reflected in a polished surface. Sometimes he is hidden among a crowd, his camera raised toward a subject no one else appears to notice.
The man is Mahir Hossain.
In one photograph, Mahir stands outside Arif's study on the night of the murder. In another, he is reflected in the glass behind Arindom's desk. In a third, he appears at the edge of the gathering where Jawer first encountered the symbol of the crossed eye. The images establish that Mahir had access to all three men, but the details are stranger than simple proximity can explain.
The plate from Arif's study shows Mahir holding a camera with a cracked lens. The plate from Arindom's office shows the same camera, with the same crack, although the photograph is dated four months earlier. The plate associated with Jawer shows the camera reflected in a mirror, but the reflection contains no photographer.
The final plate in the collection has no date. It shows Mahir standing alone before a blank wall. His camera is pointed directly at the viewer. Behind him, written in the dust, are the words:
THE FIRST WITNESS WAS NEVER ARIF.
The phrase matches the message that appeared beside the pendant.
Beneath it, almost invisible, is a second sentence:
HE WAS ONLY THE FIRST WITNESS YOU WERE GIVEN.
[ The Anonymous Letter ]
The anonymous letter was not delivered through the newspaper's usual correspondence system. Its paper came from a batch purchased by Mahir for photographic records. The ink contains traces of a chemical solution used in his studio. The handwriting imitates the investigator's, including a distinctive break in the final stroke of certain letters.
The letter's wording was designed to lead the investigator toward Durjo, then away from him, then toward the notebook, and finally toward the hidden chamber. Its statements were carefully chosen. Some were true. Some were technically true but deliberately misleading. Others described events that had not yet occurred when the letter was supposedly written.
A note in Mahir's private register refers to the letter as the first exposure.
The next entry reads:
The investigator must believe the discovery is their own. A guided hand leaves fingerprints. An awakened suspicion leaves a trail.
The letter was not sent to prevent a crime. It was sent to produce a particular investigation, with particular discoveries, in a particular order.
But the final paragraph does not appear in the surviving copy. It is present only as an impression on the page beneath it, as though the words were written on a sheet that no longer exists.
The impression reads:
WHEN THE INVESTIGATOR REACHES THE CHAMBER, THE CHAMBER WILL HAVE ALREADY REACHED THE INVESTIGATOR.
[ The Blank Notebook ]
The notebook recovered from Jawer appears empty. Under angled light, however, its pages reveal impressions that correspond to the symbols in the national ledger. The marks are not a simple substitution cipher. Their positions change depending on which page is placed beneath them, as if the notebook were designed to be read through the pressure left by another document.
The impressions reveal routes between the cult's local cells, names of intermediaries, and dates associated with staged incidents. They also contain references to photographs that have not been found.
One sequence ends with three dates: 5 October, the day Arif died; the date of Arindom's death in custody; and 14 October, the date written beside the investigator's claim to have killed Jawer.
The sequence does not identify the dates as past or future. Instead, it marks each with the same symbol used for an exposure that has not yet developed.
The final page bears a sentence in the investigator's handwriting:
I KILLED JAWER CHAKROVARTY ON THE FOURTEENTH OF OCTOBER, 1887.
No body has been recovered. No witness has confirmed the killing. The investigator cannot remember committing it.
The sentence is not written in the same ink as the other impressions. It is fresh.
When the notebook is closed, a faint sound comes from within it: the slow, deliberate click of a camera shutter.
[ The Unfinished Photograph ]
A glass plate found inside a locked drawer depicts the hidden chamber from an angle that should be physically impossible. It shows the national ledger, the photographic apparatus, the empty chair, and the investigator standing with their back to the camera.
The plate also shows Mahir.
He stands on the opposite side of the room, facing the investigator. His expression is calm. One hand rests on the investigator's shoulder.
The investigator was alone when the plate was discovered.
The plate's date is 14 October 1887, but the chemical development marks indicate that it was prepared before that day. The plate is labelled with a name that has been scraped away. Under magnification, the remaining letters resemble the investigator's name.
The most disturbing detail appears only when the plate is held before a flame. The room remains visible, but the figures vanish. In their place are dark outlines of the same people, standing in positions that do not match their bodies in the original image.
Mahir's outline is facing the wall.
The investigator's outline is facing the camera.
And the empty chair has a shadow.
